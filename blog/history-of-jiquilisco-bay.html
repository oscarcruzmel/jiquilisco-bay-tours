HISTORY & HERITAGE

History of Jiquilisco Bay

The story of the bay is inseparable from water, mangroves, fishing communities and the growing recognition of its ecological importance.

A working coastal landscape

Long before the bay became promoted as an ecotourism destination, its waterways and coastal resources supported local livelihoods. Ramsar documentation lists fishing, shellfish harvesting, aquaculture, salt production, cattle raising and coconut cultivation among important economic activities around the wetland.

Puerto communities and the water

Places such as Puerto El Triunfo developed a close relationship with the bay. Today its pier remains one of the best-known departure points for boat trips, fishing and access toward islands and mangrove channels.

2005: international wetland recognition

On October 31, 2005, the Complejo Bahía de Jiquilisco was designated a Wetland of International Importance under the Ramsar Convention. The listed site covers 63,500 hectares in Usulután. The designation recognizes a complex of brackish water, mangroves, estuaries, channels, dunes, beaches, islands and connected forests.

Why conservation became central

The bay's mangroves provide wildlife habitat while also stabilizing soil, reducing erosion and contributing to flood protection. The area is significant for coastal birds and sea turtles, making conservation part of its modern identity.

Jiquilisco Bay today

The bay remains both a home and workplace for local communities and a destination for visitors seeking nature. Tourism can support that relationship when it respects the ecosystem, uses local services and avoids turning sensitive habitat into a disposable attraction.

A living history: Jiquilisco Bay's story is not only about a date or designation. Fishing, mangroves, islands, ports and coastal families continue to shape it every day.
