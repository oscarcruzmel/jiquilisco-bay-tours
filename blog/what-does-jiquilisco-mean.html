What Does “Jiquilisco” Mean?

The story behind El Salvador's Bay of Stars—and the names that connect this remarkable coastal landscape to nature and Indigenous history.

Bahía de Jiquilisco, Isla de Méndez. Photo: Metztli / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0.

A name with more than one historical interpretation

Jiquilisco is more than a place name. It carries layers of Indigenous language, landscape and history. Modern Salvadoran environmental sources interpret Jiquilisco as “place of the jiquiles,” referring to wild fruits associated with the region. Other historical traditions connect the older spelling Xiquilisco with xiquilit or jiquilite, the plant associated with the production of indigo, or añil. Because etymologies can change across sources and time, the most responsible way to tell the story is to preserve these interpretations rather than present one as the only possible meaning.

Xiriualtique: the land of the stars

An older name associated with the wider landscape is Xiriualtique (also written in historical sources with variant spellings). El Salvador's Ministry of Environment describes Xiriualtique as meaning “the land of the stars.” Other historical descriptions refer poetically to the area as the Bay of the Stars. The name remains alive today in the Xiriualtique–Jiquilisco Biosphere Reserve.

Why “Bay of the Stars”? The name evokes the coastal night sky and the relationship between people, water and landscape. On a calm night, the idea of stars above a broad tropical bay feels especially fitting.

Jiquilite, indigo and the blue that shaped history

Jiquilite (Indigofera suffruticosa) is one of the plants traditionally used to produce indigo dye. Añil became an important agricultural product in El Salvador and across Central America. That history helps explain why some interpretations of Jiquilisco have long connected the name with jiquilite and indigo cultivation.

Indigofera suffruticosa (jiquilite). Photo: Forest & Kim Starr / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 3.0.

A name connected to nature

Whether we focus on jiquiles, the historical association with jiquilite and indigo, or Xiriualtique and the stars, the names share something important: they describe a place whose identity is inseparable from the natural world. Water, plants, mangroves, islands, wildlife and coastal communities have shaped Jiquilisco for generations.

Bahía de Jiquilisco today

Today the bay remains one of El Salvador's most important coastal landscapes. Its mangroves, estuaries, channels, beaches, islands and communities form a living environment rather than a single attraction. El Salvador's Ministry of Environment identifies the area as the country's greatest concentration of mangroves, while the bay's international Ramsar designation recognizes its ecological importance.
